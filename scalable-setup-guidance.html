Intuit Mailchimp · Onboarding

Scalable systems for setup guidance.

Experiment data showed that the homepage setup tasks were not driving engagement. I moved over to the homepage space temporarily to help address this issue. What I quickly discovered was that the business had no clear pattern for helping users beyond initial setup. What started as a project to improve new-user setup became an effort to define a global guidance system that could surface relevant next steps anywhere in Mailchimp, at any point in a user’s journey.

38% Increase in domain authentications

My role
Lead Designer
  • Reframed the problem from "guided tour" to "scalable task system," shifting the product strategy for the entire setup space
  • Ran competitive analysis across central SaaS platforms to identify guidance patterns and establish design benchmarks
  • Synthesized three years of existing qualitative research alongside new FullStory behavioral analysis
  • Designed and ran three rounds of usability testing with 8 users, iterating the experience from prototype to MVP
  • Coordinated across 6 platform teams to ensure the tasklist system could scale to surface guidance from any part of the product
  • Presented findings and future vision to C-suite leadership, securing investment for phased rollout
The problem

Critical setup tasks were being missed

The reframe that changed the scope of the project

New users were dropping off on critical setup actions on the homepage such as domain authentication, list imports, and integrations connections.

These setup tasks directly predict whether a user will send their first email. And "first send" is the strongest signal for retention and paid conversion.

The original brief was focused on a specific surface, a small setup carousel on the homepage. The reframe I brought to the team was that this isn't just a new-user problem. Users at any stage of their Mailchimp journey can fall behind on setup tasks that directly predict whether they'll send their first email, convert to paid, or get enough value to stay. What we needed wasn't a better carousel. It was a scalable guidance system. Persistent, contextual, and accessible to any user, at any point.

Research & analysis

Gathering data + cross-team research to guide explorations

Competitive research, user segments, and cross-team alignment

I started with a competitive analysis across onboarding patterns in SaaS and marketing platforms to understand how other products handled guidance and task discoverability. Because I already owned the account setup space, I could draw on existing knowledge about user segments and business verticals, what data we collected, and which tasks users consistently missed or had low awareness of. That existing context let me move faster and make stronger recommendations than starting from scratch would have allowed.

The cross-team design challenge was equally important. The tasklist needed to surface actions owned by product teams across the platform, not just in setup. To solve this, I created a shared working space where designers from other parts of the product could contribute the actions users needed to take in their areas but that weren't being surfaced anywhere. I also collaborated with Intuit's QuickBooks onboarding team to learn from their approach to personalization and understand how cross-product signals could strengthen task relevance from day one.

My Strategy

Four principles that guided the system design

1

Scalable System

Build reusable patterns, not one-off widgets. Create a framework that grows with the product.

2

Smart Personalization

Use account data to show relevant tasks. Different businesses need different guidance.

3

Data-Driven Guardrails

Protect what works. Let metrics guide changes, not assumptions.

North Star Goal

Drive 1-3 critical setup completions per user in their first 30 days, boosting retention and long-term success.

Where should the tasklist live?

Placement had downstream implications for discoverability, trust, and how users would relate to the feature long-term — not just at first launch.

Option A

Top nav

An icon next to the global Help button. Tasklist appears on click. Visible from anywhere in the product, but competes with other global nav elements for attention.

Option B

Left nav

Positioned directly above the Create/global navigation. Always accessible by one click. Allows the user to have the menu open without having to disrupt their current workflow.

Option C

Homepage module

A persistent module on the homepage. High visibility for first visits, but not globally accessible. Some may also find a pinned checklist frustrating if they don't intend to complete everything.

Option D

Right rail

A full length sidebar on the right side of the screen. Users may or may not engage - would need compelling copy to encourage usage.

User testing

Non-biased user testing

What testing revealed about attention and intent

I ran multiple usability tests on 4 of the top design options (narrowed down through regular syncs with my PM and Growth design team members), asking users to "tell us what they would do first" and not hinting at what we were testing. The tests surfaced a critical insight - users consistently expected the tasklist to be clearly visible and not buried or hidden behind an icon.

Testing also revealed that completion messaging mattered more than we expected. Users who saw a progress indicator were measurably more likely to return and complete a second task within the same session. That finding directly shaped the MVP's visual design.

MVP launch and results

Controlled experiment, held for 30 days post-launch

After user testing, I moved the tasklist to the top of the homepage to give it the prominence users were asking for. I explored ways to shorten the height to keep the high-performing creation actions (ex: "Top templates for e-commerce") above the fold.

The MVP targeted users in their first 30 days and surfaced the tasklist as a persistent module on the homepage dashboard. The tasklist contained 5-6 personalized tasks based on the user's industry and account data, with a progress indicator to encourage completion.

Cross-team systems complexity

To enable this work, I collaborated with 6+ teams across Mailchimp to ensure alignment on validation, completion logic, appropriate tasks and consistency with copy. I built a flexible component library that would flex for other use cases.

Engineering handoff & extensibility

Scaling the system

The same infrastructure, extended to Automations.

From setup to the full product lifecycle

Once the tasklist system proved its value in setup, the natural next step was extending it to Automations, where users faced a similar problem: high-value actions buried behind discovery friction. The content model we had built was already designed for this. Adding Automations guidance required no new infrastructure, only new tasks. That was the point. s

This expansion validated the core architectural decision: designing a shared content model across platform teams meant the system could grow without growing the design and engineering investment proportionally. Each new area the tasklist entered cost less than the one before it.

One system. Any part of the product.

The tasklist didn't just scale to Automations. It established the pattern for how Mailchimp surfaces contextual guidance across the platform. Any team that wants to help users onboard now has a channel to do it, without building something new.

Future vision

Looking ahead

Evolving from a setup module into a persistent, AI-powered contextual assistant

My long-term vision was to evolve this from an in-page module into a global, persistent assistant that uses business profile data to surface the right task at the right time, anywhere in the product, not just during new onboarding. What excited me most was the ability for AI to surface highly personalized tasks based on the robust business profile data we had gathered about a business.

It could personalize recommendations using the user's business vertical, website/branding, the time of year, audience data, and more. Imagine that a veterinary practice in Spring could get flea and tick seasonal campaign prompts. A boutique with a lapsed audience gets a re-engagement workflow. The product stops being a tool users have to learn and starts being a partner that already understands their business.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

The reframe was the work

The original brief was to improve a setup carousel. The most impactful thing I did on this project was expand the scope to build a scalable guidance system.

Designing for scale means designing for other teams

Coordinating across 6+ platform teams wasn't a process overhead — it was core to the design. The shared content model and flexible component library I built meant that when the system expanded to Automations, it required no new infrastructure. That architectural decision was also a design decision: the more extensible the system, the more users it could eventually reach without proportional cost.

Non-biased testing surfaces what reviews miss

By asking users "tell us what you'd do first" without hinting at what we were testing, we learned things a design review never would have caught. Users consistently wanted to see the setup tasks front and center, directly shaping the MVP's visual design in ways that contributed to engagement in the experiment.

The architecture decision is the long-term value

The results — +38% domain authentication, +37% SMS adoption, +15% list imports — validated the MVP. But the more durable win was a system any product team could plug into without starting from scratch. Designing for that future was a bigger contribution than any single screen.
